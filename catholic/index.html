The Living System of Catholic Formation

Through the lens of autopoiesis, social neuroscience and distributed cognition

This project frames Catholic spiritual formation as a non-biological autopoietic system, reinterpreting discipleship not as knowledge transfer, but as continuous participation in an operationally closed, self-producing environment. By synthesizing biological autopoiesis, organizational cybernetics and social neuroscience, the model demonstrates how the system recursively regenerates the living Body of Christ across generational member turnover. Ultimately, the framework serves as a practical model for ecclesial architecture, illustrating how replacing clerical single points of failure with distributed formational networks preserves institutional identity and systemic resilience.

    Description

    Choose an event (a relic tour, a papal visit, a visiting choir, World Youth Day or a Eucharistic procession) and tap the sphere. A wave of enthusiasm ripples across the membrane and through the members it reaches, who glow gold. While it lasts they come to Mass more often, occasional attenders are more likely to return, and newcomers arrive. It fades over weeks. What remains depends on the parish: enthusiasm becomes lasting bonds only through the groups and practice that can absorb it. Six months later, the log reports how much stayed. Try the same event under closed leadership and compare.

    The whole Church is modeled in six regions: North America, Latin America, Africa, Europe, Asia and Oceania. Each region’s Catholics are carried by five-year age group and change every year through infant baptisms (UN births to Catholic parents, times a fitted rate of baptism), deaths (UN life tables), adult and child baptisms of people not raised Catholic, people who stop identifying as Catholic, and migration (UN net migration, weighted by the Catholic share of immigrants from each origin). The model keeps two counts: self-identified Catholics, and living baptized Catholics, who include those who no longer identify. A third, the count in Vatican-style records, is larger because records rarely remove people who have died or emigrated. Priests and women religious are carried by age too, entering from young self-identified Catholics and leaving through death and departure. The rates were fitted to Vatican, survey and census data from 1978 to 2024 and tested on 2000 to 2024 after fitting to the years before; projections hold them at their 2024 levels.

    In the whole-Church view, each region is a cluster of points, one for about every 10 million Catholics, counted either way. Its size shows rooted robustness: the weight of institutions built up over generations. Its depth of green shows vitality: new life coming in. Its slow heartbeat, a gentle brightening and dimming, shows whether it is regenerating faster than it decays, and the rings that spread from it each Sunday show how many are at Mass. Events can be brought here too: tap the space around the continents.

    Three lenses

    Autopoiesis. The parish is read as a system that produces itself. Over a few decades most members are replaced through births, deaths, converts and moves, yet the pattern of relations persists. Sacraments produce specific relations, roles and groups hold the body’s shape, and the calendar orders when the parts come together. Events trigger change, but the body’s own structure decides what lasts, which is why the same papal visit leaves a mark in one parish and passes through another.

    Distributed cognition. Faith here is not held in any one head. It is carried across people, practices and shared artefacts: the rite, the readings, the calendar, the habits of a choir or a Bible study. Formation happens as the community performs these together, what Friedrich and colleagues call the competent performance of the community as a whole. In the model, no single member holds the parish’s life; it lives in the network of ties, which is why losing a group affects people who never belonged to it.

    Organizational cybernetics. In Beer’s Viable System Model, the liturgical calendar is the coordinating function: it keeps every group in step without anyone giving orders. Internal regulation sits with the pastor and bishop, but by Ashby’s law no single regulator can match a whole community’s variety. Where one role carries a function on which much else depends, as the pastor, the spiritual director and OCIA do here, the model shows a single point of failure. The remedy is distribution and subsidiarity, so the hierarchy serves identity and coordination rather than acting as a bottleneck.

    Attendance is not fixed. Each year some regular attenders slip into occasional practice and some occasional attenders return, often at a marriage, a child’s baptism or a great feast. Formation in childhood, a parent’s practice, spiritual direction, the young adults group and, as a hypothesis, the reverence of the liturgy all shift those chances.

    Leave it running for decades and the parish remakes itself: people are born, age, join groups, leave, die and are replaced by births, converts and people moving in. The members change while the organization persists: autopoiesis.

    The parish also produces its own organization. Catechists, lay ministers, music ministers, spiritual directors, deacons, the parish council and school leadership are members, not fixed nodes: people who have taken part in formation for long enough become eligible, existing ministers train them, and the council calls them forward. Each function’s capacity decays as people step down, move or leave, and is renewed only by this production from within. When a role has no one, long-standing members of its groups can keep them going at reduced strength. Groups weaken when the roles that lead them do. The closure measure counts the functions that have a working production pathway inside the parish; the pastor alone is supplied from outside, by the diocese. Use the self-production tests to remove members and leaders, or to cut a pathway, and watch whether the organization regenerates.

    Watch the age pyramid and the regeneration ratio. A parish can hold its numbers for years while aging toward a point where it can no longer replace itself.
